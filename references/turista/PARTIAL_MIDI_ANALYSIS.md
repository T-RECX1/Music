# TURiSTA — Partial MIDI Reconstruction Analysis

> Source: Tatum's uploaded partial reconstruction of Bad Bunny's “TURiSTA.” This study intentionally distinguishes what is encoded in the MIDI from arrangement details Tatum reports are missing.

## Source files supplied

- `TURiSTA_Guitar.mid`
- `TURiSTA_Ukulele.mid`
- `TURiSTA_background_atmospheric_synthpad_thing_.mid`
- `TURiSTA_Bad_Bunny_Vocals_turned_MIDI.mid`

## Important source limitations

The reconstruction is intentionally incomplete.

- Some real-song **guitar stops/rests are not entered**, so continuous MIDI does not prove continuous guitar in the released arrangement.
- There is **no bass MIDI**.
- Tatum reports that the bass broadly follows the guitar's single-note anchor events, using related rather than necessarily identical notes.
- The real arrangement contains **woodblocks and other rhythmic/percussive material** not represented here.
- The synth/pad is mainly an opening color and returns selectively rather than behaving as a continuous pad.
- The ukulele is also introduced at the beginning and returns at selected points.
- The vocal MIDI is an audio-to-MIDI pitch conversion. It is useful for macro-contour and recurrence, but tiny notes can represent slides, vibrato, consonants, or pitch-detection artifacts rather than deliberate composed pitches.

Therefore this file should be used as a **structural/emotional/catchiness study**, not as an authoritative transcription of every detail of the master.

# 2. The guitar pattern is partly a rhythm section

Tatum describes a central guitar behavior approximately as:

**single note → chord → chord → chord → single note → chord → chord → chord**

The exact MIDI varies, but this conceptual split is essential.

The guitar is doing two jobs:

## A. Single-note anchor

The isolated note acts like:

- a bass pointer
- a phrase marker
- a harmonic reset
- a rhythmic downstroke / punctuation event

## B. Repeated chord hits

The following chord repetitions act like:

- groove
- harmonic shimmer
- timekeeping
- response to the anchor

The emotional effect is not merely “acoustic guitar chords.”

It is a **punctuated ostinato**.

A useful abstraction is:

> **ANCHOR → texture texture texture**

This pattern is memorable before the listener consciously analyzes the pitches.

Reusable principle:

> **A harmonic instrument can create a rhythmic hook by alternating one structurally important note with repeated chord responses.**

# 3. Why the missing bass matters even though the guitar already implies it

Tatum reports that the bass broadly follows the guitar's isolated anchor-note moments.

That means the arrangement likely reinforces the distinction between:

- **anchor events** = low-frequency structural moments
- **chord repetitions** = upper/midrange rhythmic texture

The bass does not need to duplicate the guitar exactly.

Its important role is to agree with the *timing and direction* of the anchor.

This creates hierarchy:

**bass + guitar single note = structural statement**

followed by:

**guitar chord hits = rhythmic/harmonic afterimage**

Reusable principle:

> **Catchiness can come from multiple instruments agreeing on the same punctuation points even when they do not play identical pitches.**

This will matter for future composition: a memorable bass does not have to be independently busy. It can strengthen the song's existing phrase grammar.

# 4. The rhythm exists even without conventional drums

It would be misleading to call TURiSTA rhythm-less simply because it is not organized around a normal kick/snare/hi-hat beat.

Its pulse is distributed across:

- repeated guitar attacks
- bass anchor points
- woodblock / clave-like ticks
- selective ukulele activity
- vocal rhythm
- other light percussion in the released arrangement

This is important for the future tropical-night piece.

The listener can have a strong bodily sense of pulse without hearing a drum kit.

Reusable principle:

> **Rhythmic identity can be distributed across pitched and unpitched layers instead of delegated to drums.**

---

# 5. The woodblock / clave-like layer is a rhythmic landmark

The woodblock sound is small, dry, and easy to locate in time.

That makes it perceptually powerful even at low volume.

It can provide:

- a tick-tock reference
- tropical / acoustic coding
- forward motion without heaviness
- a stable clock against more relaxed guitar/vocal phrasing

This is especially useful at night-like tempos because it prevents the music from becoming amorphous.

The woodblock is therefore not just decoration.

It helps define the **temporal environment**.

Reusable principle:

> **A tiny dry percussive sound can make a sparse atmospheric arrangement feel rhythmically alive without making it feel drum-driven.**

# 6. Pad and ukulele behave as colors, not permanent foundations

The supplied MIDI includes:

- an atmospheric synth/pad
- ukulele

But Tatum notes that these are most obvious at the beginning and reappear selectively.

That is structurally useful.

If those timbres were present constantly, the listener would acclimate to them.

By appearing and disappearing, they become **section markers**.

## Pad

Likely emotional job:

- horizon / air
- softness behind the acoustic foreground
- nighttime width
- slight dreaminess

## Ukulele

Likely emotional job:

- warmer tropical identity
- high-frequency acoustic texture
- light rhythmic articulation
- contrast against the fuller guitar

Reusable principle:

> **Timbres can define sections simply by returning; they do not need new notes to create development.**

# 9. The “anchor + three responses” pattern is itself a hook

For catchiness analysis, do not look only at the sung melody.

The guitar/bass architecture creates a repeating **prosodic pattern**:

**statement → response → response → response**

Then repeat.

This gives the listener predictable grammar.

The listener can anticipate where the next structural note will happen even before knowing its pitch.

That is one of the fundamental ingredients of catchiness:

> **prediction with enough variation to stay interesting**

If a future melody is excellent but the accompaniment does not create recognizable phrase grammar, the whole track may still feel less memorable.

---

# 10. Catchiness has multiple nested layers here

TURiSTA should be studied as several hooks operating simultaneously.

## Layer 1 — rhythmic hook

Single-note anchor + repeated chord responses.

## Layer 2 — timbral hook

Dry woodblock/clave-like tick and acoustic guitar identity.

## Layer 3 — harmonic hook

The repeating relationship between anchor notes and chord responses.

## Layer 4 — vocal hook

Recurring contour/rhythm in the sung line.

## Layer 5 — arrangement hook

Pad/ukulele colors disappear and return.

The important lesson is:

> **A catchy song does not need one isolated “catchy melody.” Several smaller predictable identities can reinforce each other.**

This may partly explain why an earlier generated track could contain a technically repeated lead and still feel less memorable: the lead had to carry too much of the identity by itself.

# 11. Vocal-to-MIDI: what is trustworthy

Because the vocal file was generated from audio, the strongest evidence is:

- broad ascending / descending contour
- repeated phrase shapes
- phrase rhythm
- register
- landing notes
- recurrence of similar cells

Less trustworthy:

- extremely short chromatic fragments
- rapid neighboring notes during sustained syllables
- tiny timing deviations caused by pitch detection
- note boundaries during slides

For the later cross-song melody study, clean the vocal line before comparing it with instrumental MIDI.

Recommended process:

1. remove extremely short converter fragments
2. merge adjacent same-pitch events
3. reduce slides to structural start/target notes where appropriate
4. preserve phrase rhythm
5. compare interval contour independent of key
6. measure repeated 3–7 note cells
7. compare where phrases repeat exactly vs approximately

This suggests a future composition should not merely ask:

> “Is my lead melody good?”

It should ask:

> **“Does the whole arrangement teach the listener a recognizable pattern within the first few bars?”**

# 13. Direct correction to the previous original-track approach

The earlier DRIFTGLASS-style approach over-weighted:

- atmosphere
- spacious intervals
- harmonic color
- late thematic payoff

and under-weighted:

- compact hook contour
- repeated rhythmic cell
- immediate phrase grammar
- accompaniment-based memorability

For the future nighttime/tropical piece, the order should be:

1. write a **small, hummable motif**
2. give it a **rhythmic fingerprint**
3. build a recognizable accompaniment punctuation pattern
4. establish it early
5. repeat it enough to become memory
6. remove or fragment it
7. use harmonic/half-step transformations
8. return it in a richer arrangement

Atmosphere should amplify identity, not substitute for it.

# 15. Reusable rules from TURiSTA

1. **Give atmospheric music a body pulse.**
2. **A single-note anchor followed by repeated chord hits can be a hook.**
3. **Let bass reinforce punctuation rather than simply duplicate guitar.**
4. **A tiny woodblock/clave-like sound can carry time without a drum kit.**
5. **Warm tropical timbre can coexist with melancholy.**
6. **Semitone darkness can sit inside acoustic warmth instead of ambient coldness.**
7. **Use timbral returns—pad, ukulele, special percussion—as arrangement markers.**
8. **Make several small hooks reinforce each other rather than forcing one lead to carry the entire song.**
9. **Establish phrase grammar early enough that the listener can predict the next event.**
10. **For audio-to-MIDI vocals, analyze macro-contour and recurrence, not every detected pitch.**
11. **Preserve deliberate silence/stops when reconstructing the final arrangement; continuous MIDI can overstate density.**
12. **Catchiness should be tested without production effects, but also with accompaniment because accompaniment itself can be part of the hook.**

# 16. Future catchiness-analysis checklist

When comparing Mice on Venus, Aquel Nap ZzZz, and TURiSTA directly, measure:

- shortest recognizable motif length
- interval contour
- rhythmic fingerprint
- proportion of stepwise vs leap motion
- half-step frequency and placement
- repeated-note frequency
- phrase symmetry/asymmetry
- phrase ending behavior
- highest/lowest-note placement
- exact motif recurrence
- approximate motif recurrence
- repetition interval in bars
- accompaniment hook recurrence
- whether bass reinforces hook timing
- whether the hook survives instrument/timbre removal
- how soon the listener first hears the central identity
- how long the hook disappears before returning
- whether the final return is exact or transformed

The final goal is not merely to identify what these songs have in common.

It is to derive a **melodic design specification** that makes the next original track memorable before adding atmosphere.
